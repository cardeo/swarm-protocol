# Run SWARM

This document explains how to run the SWARM protocol.

SWARM can be practiced by a human alone or with assistance from AI systems.

The protocol ensures that human judgment remains inside the decision loop even when AI tools are used.

SWARM is not a workflow or productivity system.

It is a loop-based thinking protocol used to explore ideas, detect signals, and produce outcomes through repeated iterations.

---

# The SWARM Loop

The protocol operates through a five-stage loop.

Spot → Weigh → Arrange → Refine → Make

Each pass through these stages is called a **loop**.

Most loops do not produce finished outcomes.
They generate momentum and new material for the next loop.

Clarity emerges through repeated loops.

---

# Running a SWARM Session

A SWARM session is a sequence of loops.

A practitioner may run a session using:

- a notebook
- a whiteboard
- a document
- a design tool
- AI-assisted tools
- any workspace that allows iteration

The goal is not to reach a conclusion quickly.

The goal is to observe which signals survive across loops.

---

# Step 1 — Spot

Capture observations without evaluating them.

Examples:

- ideas
- friction points
- patterns
- user behaviors
- design opportunities
- anomalies
- questions

Guidelines:

- capture quickly
- avoid filtering
- avoid solving

This stage expands the observation field.

AI systems may assist by generating options or surfacing observations.

Human practitioners decide what is worth noticing.

---

# Step 2 — Weigh

Evaluate the observations captured in the previous stage.

At this point some observations may begin to look like potential signals.

Questions to consider:

- Does this observation repeat?
- Does it connect to other observations?
- What impact could this have on the problem being explored?
- Would addressing this materially change the product, system, or outcome?
- Is this noise or something worth pursuing?

Weak observations often collapse at this stage.

Observations that persist begin to gain weight and may evolve into signals across repeated loops.

AI systems may help summarize or cluster observations, but the practitioner decides what matters.

---

# Step 3 — Arrange

Organize observations into meaningful structure.

Possible arrangements include:

- clusters
- lists
- diagrams
- maps
- patterns
- workflows
- systems
- the natural order of events or actions

Multiple valuable arrangements may emerge at this stage.

The goal is not perfect structure.

The goal is to reveal relationships between observations and surface possible directions for further exploration.

Structure often changes across loops as understanding improves.

---

# Step 4 — Refine

Clarify which direction is worth pursuing.

At this stage the practitioner reviews the structures created in Arrange and determines which one should move forward into execution.

Actions may include:

- removing weak observations
- simplifying structures
- clarifying relationships
- selecting one cluster or direction to pursue
- identifying which ideas should be parked for later

It is common for multiple high-value arrangements to exist.

When this happens, one direction is selected for immediate focus while others are **parked** for future exploration.

These parked directions are not discarded. They may become important in later loops as signals strengthen or context changes.

Refinement therefore focuses attention without collapsing the broader exploration.

---

# Step 5 — Make

Produce a concrete output from the current loop.

The purpose of this step is momentum.

The output does not need to be polished or complete.  
It only needs to move the work forward and generate new observations for the next loop.

Examples include:

- a sketch or visual exploration
- a prototype
- a user interview
- a short experiment
- a written concept
- a workflow draft
- a small product decision
- a musical idea or composition fragment

Momentum is more important than completeness.

Across repeated loops, outputs begin to reveal patterns.  
These patterns may eventually become signals.

Outputs produced in one loop often become inputs for the next loop.

---

# Run Multiple Loops

SWARM relies on repetition.

A typical session may involve several loops.

Example sequence:

Loop 1  
Spot → Weigh → Arrange → Refine → Make

Loop 2  
Spot → Weigh → Arrange → Refine → Make

Loop 3  
Spot → Weigh → Arrange → Refine → Make

Signals strengthen when they persist across loops.

Signals that disappear were likely noise.

---

# Working With AI Systems

AI tools may assist inside the loop.

Examples include:

- generating ideas
- summarizing observations
- organizing information
- identifying possible patterns
- suggesting potential signals

However, AI systems do not determine meaning.

The practitioner remains responsible for:

- validating whether a pattern is truly a signal
- interpreting what the signal means
- deciding what matters

AI can surface possibilities.

Humans confirm whether those possibilities are real.

---

# Observing Signals

Signals are patterns that persist across loops.

They rarely appear during the first loop.

Signals emerge when observations:

- repeat across loops
- connect to other observations
- survive refinement
- consistently produce useful outputs

Signals are therefore discovered through repetition rather than declared early.

---

# When a Protocol Emerges

Over time, practitioners may notice that certain loops produce reliable outcomes.

When this happens, a repeatable structure has emerged.

That structure becomes a **protocol**.

Protocols are discovered through use rather than imposed before work begins.

Patterns and protocols can be documented in this repository as they emerge.

---

# Summary

Running SWARM means repeating the loop:

Spot → Weigh → Arrange → Refine → Make

through multiple passes.

Clarity forms through repetition.

Signals appear when observations survive multiple loops.

Human judgment remains responsible for interpreting meaning and deciding what matters.
